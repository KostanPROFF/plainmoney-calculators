The compound interest formula

For a single deposit, A = P(1 + r/n)nt, where P is the principal, r the annual rate, n the number of compounding periods per year and t the number of years. Regular contributions are added each month and then earn interest for the rest of the period.

The rule of 72

Divide 72 by your interest rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. At 7%, money doubles in about 10 years.

Worked examples

Example: $5,000 plus $200 a month at 6%

Starting with $5,000 and adding $200 every month at 6% compounded monthly, you would have about $160,900 after 25 years. You deposit $65,000, so about $95,900 is interest.

After 15 years the same plan holds about $70,400, of which $29,400 is interest. The last ten years add more than twice as much as the first fifteen, which is compounding at work.

Frequently asked questions

What is compound interest?

It is interest earned on both your deposits and on interest already earned. Over time the interest itself grows, so balances rise faster the longer money stays invested.

Does compounding frequency matter?

A little. Daily compounding earns slightly more than monthly or yearly at the same rate, but the difference is small. The rate, the amount you add and the number of years matter far more.

What is the difference between APR and APY?

APR is the simple yearly rate. APY includes the effect of compounding, so it is slightly higher. Savings accounts usually advertise APY.

Where can I earn compound interest?

High-yield savings accounts, certificates of deposit, money market funds and bonds pay interest. Stock investments grow when dividends are reinvested and prices rise, which works in a similar way over the long term.
