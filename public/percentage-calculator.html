Percentage formulas

Percent of a number: multiply the number by the percent and divide by 100. 15% of 80 is 80 × 15 / 100 = 12.

What percent one number is of another: divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100. 30 is 30 / 120 × 100 = 25% of 120.

Percent change: subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value and multiply by 100. Going from 50 to 65 is (65 − 50) / 50 × 100 = a 30% increase.

Percent change vs. percentage points

When a rate rises from 4% to 5%, it went up 1 percentage point, but the rate itself increased by 25%. News reports and loan offers often mix these up, so check which one is meant.

Percent versus percentage points

When a rate changes, the difference can be described two ways. If a mortgage rate rises from 6% to 7%, it went up by one percentage point, but by 16.7 percent, because 1 is 16.7% of 6. News reports and loan offers sometimes mix the two, so check which one is meant.

Stacked discounts do not add up

A sale of 20% off with an extra 10% off at checkout is not 30% off. The second discount applies to the already reduced price: a $100 item becomes $80, then $72. The total discount is 28%. To combine discounts, multiply the remaining shares: 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.72, so you pay 72% of the original price.

Increases and decreases are not symmetric

A 50% drop followed by a 50% gain does not get you back to where you started. $100 falls to $50, and a 50% gain on $50 only brings it to $75. To recover from a 50% loss you need a 100% gain. This matters for investments: losses hurt more than equal-sized gains help.

Working backward from a total

To find the original amount before tax or a markup was added, divide by one plus the rate. A receipt total of $54 with 8% sales tax had a pre-tax price of $54 / 1.08 = $50. To undo a discount, divide by one minus the rate: a $72 price after 10% off was originally $72 / 0.90 = $80. Taking 8% off $54 gives $49.68, which is a common mistake.

Everyday uses

A raise from $62,000 to $65,000 is a 4.8% increase. A test with 42 of 50 answers right scores 84%. Rent of $1,500 on $5,000 of take-home pay takes 30% of the budget, and a $4.00 item that now costs $4.60 has gone up 15%.

Easy ways to get it wrong

For percent change, divide by the old value, not the new one. Percentages that apply to different bases, such as stacked discounts, cannot simply be added. And when comparing rates, keep percent changes and percentage point changes apart.

Worked examples

Example 1: a sale price

A $79.99 jacket is 25% off. The discount is 79.99 × 25 / 100 = $20.00, so you pay $59.99.

Example 2: a raise

Your salary goes from $52,000 to $55,640. The increase is $3,640, and 3,640 / 52,000 × 100 = a 7% raise.

Example 3: a test score

You answered 42 of 48 questions correctly. 42 / 48 × 100 = 87.5%.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate a percentage of a number?

Convert the percent to a decimal by dividing by 100, then multiply. For 20% of 150, multiply 150 by 0.20 to get 30.

How do I calculate percent increase?

Subtract the original value from the new value, divide by the original value and multiply by 100. A price that goes from $40 to $50 rose by 10 / 40 × 100 = 25%.

Why does a 50% drop need a 100% gain to recover?

Percent change is measured from the starting value. If $100 falls 50% to $50, getting back to $100 means adding $50, which is 100% of the new $50 base.

How do I add a percentage to a number?

Multiply by 1 plus the percent as a decimal. To add 8% sales tax to $25, multiply 25 by 1.08 to get $27.
