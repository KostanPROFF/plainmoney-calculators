The 28/36 rule

Most lenders want your housing payment (principal, interest, taxes and insurance) to stay under 28% of gross monthly income, and all debt payments together under 36%. Some loan programs, including many FHA and conventional loans, allow a total debt ratio up to 43% or higher with strong credit. This calculator uses whichever limit is lower for you.

What raises your budget

Paying off a car loan or credit card frees room under the 36% limit dollar for dollar. A larger down payment lowers the loan, and each 1 percentage point drop in the mortgage rate adds roughly 10% to the price you can afford. Remember to keep cash for closing costs, usually 2% to 5% of the price.

Worked examples

Example: $90,000 income with a car payment

A household earning $90,000 a year has $7,500 a month before taxes. The 28% housing limit allows $2,100 a month. With a $400 car payment, the 36% total debt limit allows $2,300 for housing, so the lower $2,100 applies. With $40,000 down, a 6.75% rate, 1.1% property tax and $1,600 a year in insurance, that supports a home price of about $300,700 and a loan of about $260,700.

If the car loan were paid off, the debt limit would rise to $2,700, but the 28% housing limit would still cap the budget. Raising the down payment or finding a lower rate are the main ways to afford more in this case.

Frequently asked questions

How much house can I afford on my salary?

A common guideline is a home price of about 3 to 4 times your annual income, but your debts, down payment and interest rate matter more. This calculator applies the 28/36 rule lenders use to turn your actual numbers into a price.

What is the 28/36 rule?

Lenders prefer that housing costs stay under 28% of your gross monthly income and that all debt payments, including housing, stay under 36%. Some programs allow a total of 43% or more with strong credit or cash reserves.

Does the calculator include closing costs?

No. Closing costs usually run 2% to 5% of the price and are paid in cash at closing, separate from the down payment. Keep that money aside, along with an emergency fund, before you settle on a price.

Should I buy at the top of my budget?

Not necessarily. The maximum a lender approves leaves little room for repairs, savings or a change in income. Many buyers aim below the limit so the payment stays comfortable.
